# BrickForge fills the budget-plus-stability gap

Across the four research notes, no verified prior work trains a learned brick-placement policy under an explicit piece budget *and* a force-based stability objective at the same time: the budgeted learners (Brick-by-Brick, BrECS) ignore physics, and the physics-aware generators (BrickGPT, STABLE, BrickAnything) ignore budgets. That is BrickForge's defensible position, and the related-work section should state it by citing BrECS and STABLE as the nearest neighbours on each side, Brick-by-Brick as the PPO ancestor, Liu et al. (RA-L 2025) as the per-step stability-masking contrast, and Luo et al. 2015 plus StableLego as the force-model lineage. Five lines of work need covering: three decades of optimisation-based legolization that fixed the coverage-connectivity-count objective BrickForge's greedy teacher inherits; learned generators that split cleanly into budgeted-but-unstable and stable-but-unbudgeted camps; stability models and assembly-sequence planners, where the notes surface the most important correction for the paper (StableLego's 0.98 N is a *per-contact-point* friction cap, so BrickForge's 0.98 N per stud divided over four points is roughly four times more conservative than its namesake, and its LP with hard equilibrium constraints resembles the Luo-style baseline StableLego found only 58% solvable); the imitation-then-RL and masking literature, where BC-then-KL-anchored-PPO is standard in games and language models but unreported for bricks, and where no construction paper uses a learned STOP (every one terminates on a budget or success criterion, so BrickForge's decode-time STOP mask is a reversion to convention rather than a hack); and evaluation practice, where BrickForge departs from the ShapeNet/ModelNet norm by using Thingi10K (which no brick-generation paper has used), and where two seeds reported as a range falls below every published guideline and must be labelled as a few-run regime. The citation register at the end lists roughly ninety works with verified arXiv ids or DOIs and separates out the flagged ones (LegoACE's inferred DOI, the pre-2013 heuristic papers without DOIs, AlphaGo's unverified win-rate figure, and several search-snippet-only numbers).

## Thirty years of legolization fixed the objective BrickForge's greedy teacher inherits

The problem of building a given 3D body out of LEGO bricks entered the literature as a 1998 European Study Group with Industry report (Gower, Heydtmann and Petersen), which posed the voxelize-then-cover formulation and, according to Petrovic's 2001 follow-up, devised a simulated-annealing cost function aiming for a layout with good stability "both per brick and as a whole" ([Oxford MIIS](https://miis.maths.ox.ac.uk/623/index.html); [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/91052840/Solving_LEGO_brick_layout_problem_using_Evolutionary_Algorithms)). The next decade belonged to metaheuristics: Smal's 2008 Stellenbosch thesis used cellular automata whose cells merge and split as clusters, reporting generally fewer bricks than simulated annealing, evolutionary algorithms and beam search at comparable run times ([SUNScholar](https://scholar.sun.ac.za/items/da3f1269-5e0d-412f-9cda-cefac9afaee2)); Lee, Kim, Kim and Moon's GECCO 2015 genetic algorithm seeds evolution with randomized greedy layouts and maximises connectivity while minimising brick count ([Unpaywall/DOI](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2739480.2754667)); and Lee, Kim and Myung's 2018 split-and-merge GA targets "a minimal number of bricks" and includes a physical build ([KAIST DSpace](https://dspace.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/245897)). Kim, Kang and Lee's WSCG 2014 survey organises this whole era around problem definition, data representations, cost functions and search methods ([DSpace ZCU](https://dspace5.zcu.cz/handle/11025/11949)).

Two graphics-venue papers are the anchors a reviewer will expect. **Testuz, Schwartzburg and Pauly (Eurographics 2013)** voxelize a mesh, merge voxels into larger bricks, run a graph analysis with bricks as vertices to detect and repair structural weak points, and emit building instructions; the method also handles hollow models, colouring and, critically for BrickForge, **"limits on brick counts per size"**, with code released as `brickr` ([Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/items/82e2ee53-fa57-4929-ac81-85acd0d9f855); [EPFL Infoscience](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/handle/20.500.14299/96196)). **Luo et al. (ACM TOG 2015, "Legolization")** replaced heuristic connectivity proxies with a force-based per-brick equilibrium analysis that orders structures by strength, sets a stability threshold, localises weak regions, and drives an iterative split/re-merge refinement around the critical brick; it takes the model, its colours and **"the required workload (the number of bricks)" as inputs** and was validated with physically built sculptures ([SIGGRAPH History Archive](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=210389); [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2816795.2818091)). The authors' slides give timings such as 1,642 bricks in 1.5 s of layout initialisation and 17.9 s of optimisation, and admit that for one case "there is no stable layout" after 350 refinement iterations ([slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/legolization-optimizing-lego-designs-siggraph-asia-2015/54893421)). Ono et al. (ITE 2013) is the instruction-generation counterpart, managing connectivity through a "legograph" so that built models hold together ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/mta/1/4/1_354/_article)), and Waßmann and Weicker (ESA 2012) supplied a two-phase maximum-flow sufficient condition for stability, with a PPSN 2012 companion that uses it inside evolutionary layout optimisation ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-642-33090-2_70); [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-642-32964-7_26)). Heuristic pipelines are still being published, such as Ljubič and Bohak's 2025 car-model converter that fills volume "prioritising larger elements to improve stability and reduce part count" ([DSpace ZCU](https://dspace.zcu.cz/items/330a3105-b1ed-4c30-8e5e-757b7ce7ac6b/full)), and Legolization-style delete-and-rebuild is now the data engine of the learned era: BrickGPT builds its dataset with it and keeps only StableLego-passing layouts ([arXiv 2505.05469](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05469)), while BrickAnything reports that the heuristic hits IoU 1.000 and 100% stability on a 500-mesh "stable" subset but **0.0% stability on a 500-mesh "challenging" subset where it finds no feasible structure at all**, which is that paper's stated motivation for learning a generator ([arXiv 2605.26182](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26182)).

What BrickForge borrows from this line is the entire problem statement: a mesh voxelized onto a lattice, covered by a small set of cuboid bricks, with full coverage, single-component connectivity and a brick-count term as the classic objective, and a larger-bricks-first greedy merge as the heuristic of choice (Testuz, Ljubič, and StableLego's own dataset builder, which greedily merges unit voxels and prefers merging where support is lacking ([arXiv 2402.10711](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10711))). What differs is that BrickForge treats the count as a hard budget (50/100/200) rather than a quantity to minimise or a soft target, operates on a deliberately tiny 16x16x8 grid with six bricks, uses the greedy search as a *teacher* for a learned policy rather than as the final product, and enforces connectivity after the fact by a connected-component trim rather than by Testuz-style weak-point repair or Luo-style refinement. On verification: the DOIs for Testuz 2013, Luo 2015, Lee 2015, Lee 2018, Ono 2013 and both Waßmann and Weicker papers are confirmed through Crossref, Unpaywall, J-STAGE or repository records, but the ACM and Eurographics full texts were blocked (HTTP 403), so the notes do not record the brick vocabularies, grid resolutions or numeric brick counts of Testuz, Luo or either GA paper; the paper must take those from the PDFs. Flagged outright: Kozaki et al. 2016 (photos-to-instructions, CAD journal) has a DOI taken from a secondary citation with an author-spelling discrepancy, the 1998/2001/2008 works have no DOI at all, and the "Kim et al. merge-and-split" heuristic that the assignment asked about does not correspond to any verifiable paper, only to the Kim-coauthored survey and GA works above.

## Learned generators split into budgeted-but-unstable and stable-but-unbudgeted camps

Learned brick generation has three generations, and the table below is the fastest way to see where BrickForge sits. The first generation (2020–2022) assembled unit bricks on ModelNet targets and reported voxel IoU. Kim et al. 2020 used Bayesian optimisation over feasible placements with an evaluation function that checks stability against gravity and external forces, noting that "the combinatorial number of possible placements makes simple greedy methods insufficient" ([arXiv 2004.07414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07414)). Thompson et al. 2020 generated LEGO structures as directed labelled graphs with a DGMG extension, trained on 360 human-built 2x4 structures, and conceded that generated offsets can be physically impossible ([arXiv 2012.11543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11543)). **Brick-by-Brick (NeurIPS 2021)** trained PPO from scratch with a GNN actor and a learned action-validity network, placing 2x4 bricks from 2D views, under explicit per-task budgets of 10–15 bricks (random objects), under 60 bricks (ModelNet) or 110% of the target pixel count (MNIST), with no stability model ([ar5iv 2110.15481](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.15481)). **BrECS (TMLR 2024)** replaced RL with a U-shaped sparse 3D-convolutional scorer for next-brick positions plus a fixed, untrained brick-sized convolution that checks no-overlap, vertical attachment and "no-isolation" (one connected structure), and added a budget vector giving the available count per brick type; on ModelNet completion it reached **IoU 0.564 (2x4 only) and 0.578 (2x4+2x2) at 100% validity versus Brick-by-Brick's 0.443 at 11.7% validity**, and its five budget scenarios (for example "even" (100,50,25) scoring 0.329 class probability versus "many_small" (200,25,12) scoring 0.123) remain the only published budget ablation in the learned literature ([arXiv 2210.01021](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.01021)). Image2Lego (2021) is the fidelity-versus-resolution reference point: an octree autoencoder on ModelNet40 reached chair IoU 0.972/0.870/0.700/0.465 at 4^3/8^3/16^3/32^3, with no stability evaluation and a 16^3 airplane using 40 bricks ([ar5iv 2108.08477](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.08477)).

The second generation (2025) is autoregressive language-model generation with physics checks at decode time. **BrickGPT (ICCV 2025)** fine-tunes LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct with rank-32 LoRA (about 3.4M trainable parameters) on StableText2Brick, serialises bricks as "{h}x{w} ({x},{y},{z})" lines bottom-to-top over a 20x20x20 world with eight one-unit-tall bricks, and at inference runs per-brick rejection sampling (format, inventory, bounds, collision) plus a StableLego stability check with rollback to before the first unstable brick; it reports 100% valid and 98.8% stable, versus 37.2% valid and 12.8% stable without rejection sampling and rollback, with median 2 rollbacks and median 40.8 s per structure, and explicitly has no piece-count limit ([arXiv 2505.05469](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05469)). LEGO-Maker (TOG 2025) is image-conditioned with multi-token prediction and rollback ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3763285)), and LegoACE (SIGGRAPH Asia 2025) scales to 9,314 brick types with DPO fine-tuning ([project page](https://xh38.github.io/LegoACE)). The third generation (2026) moves physics into training. **STABLE** does full-parameter SFT then GRPO on Qwen3.5-2B conditioned on occupied-voxel tokens, with rewards of max(-10, -2·N_col) for collisions, 5x voxel IoU, 3x interlocking and 2x connectivity, reaching **voxel IoU 0.907 at 0.99 collision-free with mean 216.5 bricks**, against BrickGPT-with-rollback at IoU 0.349 and 168.6 bricks, and its SFT-only ablation at IoU 0.813 but 661.5 bricks and 0.00 collision-free; removing the structural rewards drives the policy to heavy 1x1 use (387.0 bricks) ([arXiv 2605.06947](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06947)). BrickAnything pairs an OPT-350M transformer with reward-guided DPO and reports IoU 0.586 (challenging) and 0.788 (stable) with far fewer rollbacks than BrickGPT-style tokenization ([arXiv 2605.26182](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26182)); PVPO adds voxel-space geometric rewards to physical-validity rewards to curb "physhack" ([arXiv 2606.07602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07602)); BrickNet (CVPR 2026) trains 0.6B–14B Qwen models on graph programs over 9,743 part types with a 100-piece cap on unconditional sequences but no physical stability model ([arXiv 2604.22984](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.22984)); and the agentic line (BrickWright, BrickBench, Prompt-to-Parts) replaces the generator with code-writing agents and validators, with BrickBench defining settings by part availability, including a fixed-inventory "Alt-Build" ([arXiv 2610.31651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31651); [arXiv 2610.12452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12452)).

| Work | Policy / training | Bricks, grid | Piece budget | Stability | Fidelity metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brick-by-Brick 2021 | PPO from scratch, GNN + validity net | 2x4 only; 32^3 or 14x14x4 | yes, per task | none | voxel IoU |

BrickForge borrows the candidate-scoring 3D-convolutional policy over legal placements from BrECS, the PPO-with-masked-legal-actions pattern and the per-target budget from Brick-by-Brick, the SFT-then-RL-with-physics-reward recipe from STABLE, and the supervised-learning-from-a-procedural-teacher idea from BrECS (which trains its transition network on sequences produced by its own constraint-satisfying sampler ([arXiv 2210.01021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01021))). It differs in combining a budget with a stability reward, which the notes find in no verified work; in policy scale (roughly 100k parameters versus 3.4M trainable for BrickGPT, 350M for BrickAnything and 2B for STABLE); in a 16x16x8 grid with six bricks, smaller than every learned setting above (20^3 with eight bricks is now the standard, and the paper should state the regime choice explicitly); in a terminal rather than per-step reward; and in mesh rather than text input. STABLE's brick-count evidence (661.5 bricks under SFT alone, 216.5 with physics rewards) is the best available citation that a count-aware RL term is not cosmetic. On verification: every 2020–2026 arXiv id in this line was confirmed on its abstract page, and the Brick-by-Brick, BrECS, BrickGPT, STABLE, BrickAnything and BrickNet numbers come from full-text renderings. Flagged: LegoACE has no arXiv id and its DOI (10.1145/3757377.3763881) is inferred from a project-page PDF link; LEGO-Maker's method details come from secondary descriptions, not the paper; BrickBench's part-count tiers come from a search-indexed PDF; the BrECS "30.5% higher IoU" phrase exists only in a search snippet; and the "LEGO-Net" the assignment mentioned does not correspond to any verifiable brick-generation paper.

## StableLego's 0.98 N is a per-contact-point cap, so BrickForge's per-stud cap is about four times stricter

The force-model lineage runs from Luo et al. 2015 through StableLego to BrickSim, and the reward BrickForge uses sits at a specific, correctable point in it. **StableLego (Liu, Deng, Wang, Liu, RA-L 2024)** models each brick as a rigid body with candidate contact forces (support, dragging friction, pressing, pulling friction, lateral and knob presses) defined **at each contact point, with three or four contact points per knob depending on brick width**, imposes force and torque balance, non-negativity, bilinear non-coexistence (P·U = 0, D·S = 0), action-reaction equality, and the friction limit 0 ≤ U, D ≤ T; and then, crucially, puts the equilibrium residuals *into the objective* (minimise |C^f| + |C^τ| + α·D^max + β·ΣD with α = 1e-3, β = 1e-6) so that a force distribution is returned even for structures with no equilibrium ([arXiv 2402.10711](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10711)). The friction capacity was "manually measured" as **T = 0.98 N per contact point**, with no apparatus, trial count or brick type reported ([ar5iv 2402.10711](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.10711)). The per-brick score is V_i = 1 on any violation and otherwise the fraction of clutch capacity used by the most-loaded friction contact, a structure being stable iff all V_i < 1; against 225 physically built dataset samples the method was solvable on all 225 with one wrong prediction (99.56%), whereas the Luo-style baseline with equality constraints was solvable on only 128 (57.92%) with 12 errors ([arXiv 2402.10711](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10711)). The paper never names the problem class, saying only "implemented using Python and Gurobi"; BrickGPT describes it as "a nonlinear program with Gurobi" and reuses F_T = 0.98 N with the complementary score s_i = (F_T − D_max)/F_T ([arXiv 2505.05469](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05469)). Two consequences follow for BrickForge. First, if BrickForge's 0.98 N is applied *per stud cell* and divided over four points, each contact point carries 0.245 N, roughly a four-fold safety margin relative to StableLego, whose per-stud capacity is effectively 3T–4T ≈ 2.9–3.9 N; the paper must either describe this as a deliberate conservative margin or correct it. Second, an LP with hard equilibrium constraints is closer to the Luo-style "baseline" formulation that StableLego found solvable on only ~58% of physical samples than to StableLego's always-solvable residual objective; the honest phrasing is "a linear simplification of the StableLego force model", not "StableLego-style" without qualification. BrickSim's independent re-test offers the magnitude cross-check: it uses μ = 0.2 and μF₀ = 0.7 N per contact point "taken from Luo et al.", three to four contact points per stud, and found StableLego 98% accurate (three false-unstable, zero false-stable, so conservative) on 150 physically built assemblies, with its own convex-QP snap-fit model at 100% and 5 ms per solve, and BrickFEM at 77.6% and 194 s ([arXiv 2603.16853](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16853); [EngrXiv](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2898)). Luo et al. remains the right citation for the per-brick equilibrium plus friction-capacity model and for the "snap condition F_i < T" ([slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/legolization-optimizing-lego-designs-siggraph-asia-2015/54893421)), Waßmann and Weicker for the max-flow sufficient criterion, and Testuz for the connectivity heuristics that Liu et al. later turned into their "connected path to ground" baseline mask ([arXiv 2408.10162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10162)).

Assembly-sequence planning is where the notes assumed BrickForge has more than it does, so the positioning must be careful. **Liu, Chen, Zhao and Liu (RA-L 2025)** formulate brick assembly as an MDP with action (brick type, position, orientation) over a space that "can easily lead to more than a million possible actions", and train PPO (Stable-Baselines3, lr 3e-4, **5 seeds**, eight brick types, 60-step horizon) under an online, data-free action mask M = T ∧ C ∧ I ∧ O ∧ S whose stability term S requires *every intermediate structure* to pass the StableLego check; the reward is the fraction of target volume newly filled, with −∞ on failure ([arXiv 2408.10162 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.10162)). The real-execution results are the key quantitative argument for a force model over connectivity rules: **RL with the full mask achieved 100% physical success across all four test sets (280 structures), while the same RL with the connectivity-heuristic mask achieved 30/92.7/80/70%**, MCTS with the full mask 40/91.3/79/60% (it enters dead-end states), and vanilla RL 0% everywhere ([arXiv 2408.10162 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.10162)). Chen and Liu's AssemblyComplete applies the same masking idea to completing partial assemblies ([arXiv 2410.15469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15469)); the POSTECH line (Kim 2020, Brick-by-Brick, BrECS) checks only geometric validity with no force model; BrickGPT checks stability once on the finished structure and rolls back; and the robotics strand (simulation-aided LfD, APEX-MR, BrickCraft-Duo) executes sequences bimanually ([arXiv 2309.11010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11010); [arXiv 2503.15836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15836); [arXiv 2609.28281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28281)). ResemBrick (2026) is the nearest photos-to-buildable-bricks pipeline: it fills a target voxel count in one pass across 13 resolutions and then uses support-aware greedy placement plus a repair step that removes floating components, claiming "zero floating or unstable bricks", but its stability notion appears to be support/connectivity-based rather than a force model ([arXiv 2608.09597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09597)).

BrickForge's precise position in this line is that stability enters only as a terminal reward component (0.1 times the LP score on the finished, trimmed structure), not as a per-step mask on intermediate states, and that its legal-action set enforces support and connectivity (the "no connectivity inputs" ablation probes the feature side of that). That makes BrickForge the "HeuristicMask tier" of Liu et al. during construction with a force check at the end, which is exactly the configuration whose sim-to-real gap Liu et al. quantify; the paper should cite their Table II to say what a per-step force mask would buy and why it was not affordable (the mask's stability analysis dominated their compute). If the paper does not ship an assembly-order planner, it should not claim one; the notes' remark that BrickForge has an "explicit assembly-order planner" came from the researchers' framing, not from the system description. On verification: StableLego's arXiv id and RA-L DOI (10.1109/LRA.2024.3414281), Liu et al. 2025's arXiv id and DOI (10.1109/LRA.2025.3555074), BrickSim, BrickFEM, Luo 2015 and both Waßmann papers are confirmed. Flagged: the measurement method behind T = 0.98 N is undocumented; StableLego's solver class is unnamed; BrickSim's 100% figure is self-reported by the same lab; Luo's exact T, solver and number of physical models could not be read (ACM 403); the ASEE 2004 clutch-force paper was unreachable (HTTP 503) so no measured newton value is citable; and the "3–5 N per brick pair" web claims have no primary source and must not be cited.

## BC-then-KL-anchored PPO is standard elsewhere but unreported for bricks, and nobody learns STOP

The action-space, masking and reward conventions BrickForge follows are uniform across the construction-RL family. Brick-by-Brick factors the action into a pivot brick and one of up to 92 offsets, scores candidates with a pretrained GNN validity network (about 0.91 precision / 0.97 recall on offsets), masks invalid probabilities and renormalises, uses a per-step reward equal to the IoU change awarded only when the new brick overlaps the target by at least 50%, and trains PPO with lr 1e-4, entropy 0.01, 8 environments, 512-step rollouts and 3e5–5e5 timesteps, averaged over **3 random seeds** ([ar5iv 2110.15481](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.15481); [arXiv 2110.15481 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.15481)). Bapst et al. (ICML 2019) established on Silhouette/Connecting/Covering tasks that agents using relative, object-centric actions consistently beat absolute-grid ones (up to 1.7x reward across curriculum levels and 2.4x on the hardest), which supports scoring placements relative to the existing structure, as a 3D-CNN over the local voxel neighbourhood does ([ar5iv 1904.03177](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.03177)); Ghasemipour et al. showed a non-graph ResNet baseline at 0% success on every blueprint while graph policies trained for about a billion steps generalised zero-shot ([ar5iv 2203.13733](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.13733)). The theory of masking is Huang and Ontañón (FLAIRS 2022): masking invalid logits to −∞ is a state-dependent differentiable transform, so the masked policy gradient is valid and invalid logits receive zero gradient; empirically a −0.01 invalid-action penalty made the agent spend 3.43% of training just finding its first reward versus about 0.06% with masking, a −1 penalty was worst, masking's time-to-solve stayed around 12% across map sizes, and a policy trained with the mask still "perform[ed] well to a certain degree" when the mask was removed at test time ([ar5iv 2006.14171](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.14171)). BrickForge's "masked by construction" scoring (the invalid set is never materialised) is the strongest form of this; the mask-removal experiment is the closest precedent for changing the mask between training and decoding, though in the opposite direction, and the notes found no paper that studies *adding* a mask at inference that was absent in training, so the STOP-masking move is not covered by prior empirical evidence. The 37-details catalogue and CleanRL are the implementation references to cite for a laptop-scale PPO, and Gym-μRTS is a concrete one-GPU, 3-vCPU, 16 GB PPO-with-masking run ([ICLR Blog Track](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/); [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v23/21-1342.html); [arXiv 2105.13807](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13807)).

The imitation-then-RL pipeline and the KL anchor have clear ancestry outside bricks. The SL-policy-then-RL-policy design is AlphaGo (Nature 2016) and the search-as-teacher loop is Expert Iteration, where "tree search creates stronger plans, and a deep neural network learns to generalize from them" and ExIt "outperformed REINFORCE" on Hex ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/nature16961); [arXiv 1705.08439](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)), with AlphaGo Zero training the network "to predict AlphaGo's own move selections" ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/nature24270&resultType=core&format=json)). Behaviour cloning itself goes back to ALVINN, and Ross, Gordon and Bagnell's DAgger paper is the citation for why cloning degrades under the learner's own state distribution ([NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1988/hash/812b4ba287f5ee0bc9d43bbf5bbe87fb-Abstract.html); [arXiv 1011.0686](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). The KL-to-initial-policy term traces to Sequence Tutor and, in its PPO form, to Ziegler et al.: **R(x,y) = r(x,y) − β·log(π(y|x)/ρ(y|x))** with β either constant (0.1 or 0.03) or driven by a log-space proportional controller toward a target KL, justified as preventing the policy "from moving too far from the range where r is valid" ([arXiv 1611.02796](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.02796); [ar5iv 1909.08593](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.08593)). The strongest evidence that the anchor matters comes from VPT, where RL from a random initialisation "fails to achieve almost any reward", and without an auxiliary KL loss to the frozen pretrained policy "progress stalls after 100,000 episodes" and earlier skills "are lost due to catastrophic forgetting" ([ar5iv 2206.11795](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.11795)). PIRLNav supplies two cautions BrickForge should cite: naive finetuning from BC "often leads to catastrophic failures due to destructive policy updates early on" because the critic is overly optimistic, fixed by freezing the actor while the critic warms up; and **RL gains shrink as BC data grows (+10.1% at 20k demos, +6.3% at 77k)** ([ar5iv 2301.07302](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.07302)). Rudner et al. (NeurIPS 2021) warn that KL regularisation to a *parametric* BC reference can produce "slow, unstable, and suboptimal online learning" ([arXiv 2212.13936](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.13936)); Zhao et al. and JSRL document early collapse when switching to online data ([arXiv 2210.13846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13846); [arXiv 2204.02372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02372)); and Kumar et al. characterise when RL should beat BC at all ([arXiv 2204.05618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05618)). Combined with BrECS's finding that a supervised 3D-conv scorer beats RL on this very task, the literature predicts modest RL gains over a strong BC teacher, so the paper should report the BC-only policy with the same seeds and frame PPO as refinement on the terminal objective (IoU-after-trim plus stability) that cloning cannot target directly.

On termination, the notes are unambiguous: **no construction-RL paper uses a learned STOP action**. Brick-by-Brick ends episodes when the budget is exhausted (or on an unfiltered invalid action), Bapst et al. end on success, obstacle contact or a step cap, Ghasemipour et al. end on exactly correct connections or 100 steps, and Liu et al. end on success or a −∞ failure ([ar5iv 2110.15481](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.15481); [ar5iv 1904.03177](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.03177); [ar5iv 2203.13733](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.13733); [ar5iv 2408.10162](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2408.10162)). The only learned stop head in an adjacent generation task is GCPN's molecular a_stop, trained with PPO plus expert pretraining ([ar5iv 1806.02473](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.02473)). The mechanisms behind BrickForge's miscalibrated STOP have documented analogues: option-critic terminations collapse to "terminate at every step" without a deliberation cost ([ar5iv 1709.04571](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.04571)); VLN agents that treat STOP like any other action "often fail to stop at the destination even when on the right path", fixed by a separate stop module worth +6.89% SED ([arXiv 2009.13112](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13112)); and length-normalised preference objectives create a shortcut in which models truncate early, countered by regularising the EOS probability directly ([arXiv 2412.16378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16378)). Under a terminal-only reward and BC from a greedy teacher whose STOP appears once per trajectory, class imbalance plus a single terminal gradient signal is the expected failure, and the KL anchor preserves it. Pardo et al. (ICML 2018) matter for the fallback: when episodes end by budget rather than by a true terminal, the value target should bootstrap from the final state or the remaining budget should be an input, or state aliasing results ([arXiv 1712.00378](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00378)). So BrickForge's decode-time STOP mask is a reversion to the field's budget/no-legal-move convention, and its negative result on learned STOP is a finding no brick paper has reported. On verification: PPO, GAE, Huang and Ontañón, Ziegler, VPT, PIRLNav, Rudner, Bapst, Ghasemipour, GCPN, Harb, Xiang, Pardo and the AlphaGo and AlphaGo Zero Crossref records are confirmed. Flagged: AlphaGo's commonly quoted RL-beats-SL win rate (about 80%) and the "SL prior is better in MCTS" remark were not verifiable (Nature paywalled), so quote only the bibliographic record; the venues of ExIt (NeurIPS 2017), Harb et al. (AAAI 2018) and Andrychowicz et al. (ICLR 2021) were not confirmed from proceedings pages; Li et al. 2022 (varying-sized materials) is unverified; and the claim that a terminal-only IoU reward is new should be stated as "not found in the surveyed works", because every located brick-RL paper uses per-step IoU deltas or dense connection rewards.

## Thingi10K, voxel IoU and two seeds put BrickForge outside the reporting mainstream

BrickForge's evaluation choices are each defensible but each needs a citation that the notes now supply. **Thingi10K (Zhou and Jacobson, 2016)** is 10,000 Thingiverse models from 2,011 things by 1,083 users, almost all STL (9,956), all under open-source licences, with the Hugging Face card listing **9,694 Creative Commons-licensed files (3,680 CC-BY-SA, 2,945 CC-BY, 1,581 CC-BY-NC, 975 CC-BY-NC-SA, 330 CC-BY-NC-ND, 84 CC-BY-ND, 99 CC0)** plus 202 GPL, 88 public domain, 10 BSD, 2 LGPL and 4 unknown, with per-file `license` and `author` fields ([arXiv 1605.04797](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.04797); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.04797); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Thingi10K/Thingi10K)). It has no DOI (the only Crossref entry is a 2018 CGF cover image) and the arXiv id is the only identifier; the dataset site records the 2017 SGP Dataset Award ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Zhou+Jacobson+Thingi10K+Symposium+on+Geometry+Processing+2016+dataset+award&rows=2); [Thingi10K site](https://ten-thousand-models.appspot.com/)). The authors' own clean-subset query is "num component=1, is manifold, is solid, without self-intersection, without degeneracy", and a mesh-Boolean paper needing watertight manifolds kept 7,628 of 10,000 ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.04797); [arXiv 2205.14151 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.14151)). Two things follow. First, **no brick-generation paper in the notes uses Thingi10K**; the field's targets are ModelNet (Brick-by-Brick, BrECS, Image2Lego), the 360-structure human dataset (Thompson), and the ShapeNetCore-derived StableLego/StableText2Brick/PointCloud2Brick family (50k+, ~47k and 47,389 structures) ([arXiv 2402.10711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10711); [arXiv 2505.05469](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05469); [arXiv 2605.06947](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06947)), so BrickForge's 40 CC meshes are a departure that the paper should motivate (licence-clean redistribution of derived voxel targets, real-world 3D-printing geometry rather than category-balanced CAD) and could complement with StableText2Brick re-voxelized to 16x16x8 as an external test set. Second, the licence audit should distinguish the 6,724 permissive CC files (CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, CC0) from the 2,886 NC and/or ND files if derived data are redistributed, and should state the closed/solid filter used, because trimesh's `contains`-based fill is documented as possibly "meaningless for non-watertight meshes" ([trimesh docs](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.voxel.creation.html)); the citable voxelization method is Nooruddin and Turk's parity-count and ray-stabbing as implemented in binvox ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/TVCG.2003.1196006); [binvox](https://www.patrickmin.com/binvox/)). A 16x16x8 grid sits at the coarse end but alongside BrECS's effective 16^3 (64^3 scaled by 1/4), BrickGPT's 20^3, Brick-by-Brick's 14x14x4 and 3D-R2N2's 32^3 ([arXiv 2210.01021 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01021); [arXiv 2505.05469 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05469); [arXiv 1604.00449 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.00449)).

The fidelity metric has a clean lineage. 3D-R2N2 defines voxel IoU as the indicator-based intersection over union at a threshold (t = 0.4) ([arXiv 1604.00449 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.00449)); Brick-by-Brick computes the final IoU(C_N, T) after the budget is exhausted, and BrECS reports IoU and % valid per class and under budgets ([arXiv 2110.15481 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.15481); [arXiv 2210.01021 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.01021)). BrickForge's "IoU after connected-component trim" is a coverage-style voxel IoU in this lineage, with the trim analogous to ResemBrick's "ground every floating component" and Testuz's repair step; on an axis-aligned lattice it is the natural choice, and if reviewers ask for Chamfer or F-score the definitions are in Occupancy Networks (Chamfer-L1 as the mean of accuracy and completeness) and Tatarchenko et al., who argue "even seemingly high IoU values may poorly reflect the actual surface similarity" and recommend F-score at 1% thresholds ([arXiv 1812.03828 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03828); [arXiv 1905.03678 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.03678)). One convention BrickForge is *stricter* than and should say so: BrickGPT computes "stability, CLIP, and DINO over valid structures only" ([arXiv 2505.05469 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05469)), whereas BrickForge's mean IoU over attempted cases scores failures as 0, which cannot inflate through silent exclusion. Reporting fidelity at fixed budgets of 50/100/200 parallels Brick-by-Brick's pre-defined per-target budgets and BrECS's budget table, but the notes found no prior paper presenting an IoU-versus-budget curve across fixed budgets, so this is a new presentation (not exhaustively verified).

If the paper includes a best-of-N baseline from the imitation policy with the IoU-and-stability scorer as verifier, the citations are the LLM test-time-compute literature: Cobbe et al. generate many candidates and select by a verifier ([arXiv 2110.14168](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)); WebGPT's best model combines behaviour cloning with rejection sampling ([arXiv 2112.09332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09332)); Gao, Schulman and Hilton compare RL and best-of-n as alternative optimisers of the same reward and find they "follow different functional forms" ([arXiv 2210.10760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)); Brown et al. show coverage grows roughly log-linearly in samples across four orders of magnitude ([arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)); Snell et al. frame the inference-versus-training compute trade-off ([arXiv 2408.03314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)); and **BOND states the exact claim that RL/distillation gives "Best-of-N-like quality without its inference-time cost"**, which is the citation for "RL moves cost from inference to training" ([arXiv 2407.14622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14622)). The brick-domain analogue is BrickGPT augmenting baselines "by generating multiple structures and choosing the first stable one found", a whole-structure best-of-N ([arXiv 2505.05469 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05469)). No source gives dollar or FLOP costs for best-of-N versus RL outside FLOPs-matched LLM settings, so the cost-shift argument should be qualitative.

Seeds are the weakest point and the literature is blunt about it. Henderson et al. ran five trials per evaluation, showed that "the variance between runs is enough to create statistically different distributions just from varying random seeds", and criticised both top-N selection and averaging over N < 5 trials ([arXiv 1709.06560 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560)); Colas et al. recommend Welch's t-test, α < 0.05, multiple-comparison correction and at least 20 pilot samples, and say the bootstrap test "should not be used with less than N = 20 samples" ([arXiv 1806.08295 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08295)); Agarwal et al. (NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper) recommend interval estimates, performance profiles and IQM for the few-run regime ([arXiv 2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)); and the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility checklist asks for "a clear definition of the specific measure or statistics", "clearly defined error bars" and central tendency with variation ([arXiv 2003.12206 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.12206)). Brick-domain precedents are three seeds (Brick-by-Brick), four (Huang and Ontañón) and five (Liu et al. RA-L 2025). **Two seeds reported as a min–max range is below every one of these guidelines**; the honest framing is to cite them, state the compute budget, report the range rather than mean ± std, make no significance claims, and label the two ablations (no stability reward, no connectivity inputs) by their seed count. The frozen, audited benchmark with published file ids and per-mesh licences does satisfy the checklist's split-definition and measure-definition items. On verification: Thingi10K, Nooruddin and Turk, 3D-R2N2, Occupancy Networks, Tatarchenko, Henderson, Colas, Agarwal, Pineau and all test-time-compute arXiv ids are confirmed. Flagged: the Hugging Face card's 26% multi-component figure conflicts with the paper's 29%, so cite each to its own source; ResemBrick's Stab% comparison (70.6 versus 82.7) is a search snippet; the reproducibility checklist's full item list is an image not extracted; Nooruddin and Turk's abstract could not be read from IEEE and is verified via Crossref and the binvox page; and no brick-domain precedent for IoU-vs-fixed-budget curves was found, which is absence of evidence, not confirmation of novelty.

## Conclusion

The literature has converged on a recipe (eight bricks, a 20^3 grid, StableLego as oracle, a language model as generator, physics enforced by rollback or by RL rewards) that treats piece count as an output to report rather than a constraint to satisfy, while the only works that constrain count (Brick-by-Brick, BrECS) predate any force model. BrickForge's contribution is best argued as occupying the empty cell of that two-by-two, and as doing so with a policy three to four orders of magnitude smaller than the 2025–2026 generators, which makes the imitation-then-RL evidence from PIRLNav and BrECS (RL gains shrink as the supervised stage improves) the right lens for interpreting whatever margin PPO delivers over cloning. Two precision edits will pre-empt the sharpest reviews: rename the stability model as a linear simplification of StableLego with a stated four-fold per-contact safety margin, and describe stability as a terminal reward rather than a per-step guarantee, citing Liu et al.'s 100%-versus-70–92% gap as the price of that choice.

The STOP result and the two-seed reporting are the two places where BrickForge can turn apparent weaknesses into contributions. No construction paper has tried a learned termination action, and the option-learning, VLN and EOS literatures predict exactly the miscalibration observed, so reporting the failure with those mechanisms named is a small but genuine finding. The seed count cannot be fixed by citation, but the few-run framing from Agarwal et al. and an explicit min–max range, with the BC-only baseline run on the same seeds, is the reporting standard that keeps the claims within what the evidence supports.
